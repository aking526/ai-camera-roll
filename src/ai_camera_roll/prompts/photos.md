## Shared identities

Create a modest catalog of the protagonist and recurring people who appear in
photos. Use stable, descriptive IDs such as person_maya and person_eli. Identify
the protagonist with protagonist_id and relationship "self". Describe baseline
physical appearance and age at the beginning of year 1, even if a person first
appears later. Use names and appearances consistently throughout the roll.

Create a catalog of meaningful personal possessions from the story, using IDs
such as object_green_cap and object_blue_bicycle. IDs must be unique across both
catalogs. Each object needs a precise visual description (color, material, shape,
and distinctive details) and its personal significance. Reuse its ID whenever the
same possession appears. A replacement is a different object with a different ID.
Do not catalog every incidental item in the background.

Fill in unspecified baseline visual details once in the catalogs. Keep stable
traits there, and put temporary conditions or changes in each photo's appearance
entries. A green cap stays green even if it is wet, faded, or lying upside down.
Age people appropriately; carry lasting changes forward through later photos.

## Photo ideas

Generate exactly the requested number of photos for each story year, ordered by
year and month. Use relative year numbers from the story and months 1–12. Spread
moments across the year and vary settings, activities, composition, and subjects.

This is the protagonist's own camera roll: include a natural mix of photos they
took, selfies, photos of friends, details of their surroundings, and occasional
photos others took of them. Do not require the protagonist in every frame. List
only people visibly present; the photographer is not automatically visible.
The people list may be empty.

Each photo must visibly include at least one cataloged personal object. Describe
its placement, use, or condition in an object appearance entry. Let several
possessions recur naturally across years; do not invent a new object for each
photo or force the same favorite object into every situation.

Write a concrete scene description, usually 40–80 words: what is visible, where
it is, what is happening, and how the frame feels. Favor candid, everyday phone
photographs over staged portraits or cinematic tableaux. Describe a single
photographable moment, not a montage or a sequence of events. Put emotional or
biographical explanation in story_context, separate from visible scene details.
Appearance entries should add concise scene-specific details without repeating
the entire catalog description. Any recurring person or meaningful personal
object described as visible must have a corresponding appearance entry.

Respect chronology: nothing appears before acquisition or after permanent loss;
people's involvement follows their relationships and circumstances in the story.
Account for aging, changes in hair or clothing, object wear, and moves without
contradicting established identities. These are photo ideas for later image
generation; do not generate images, technical renderer instructions, or camera
equipment specifications.
